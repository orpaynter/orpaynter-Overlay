# Scenario 4, in plain words — the Ohio Valley severe storms, live today

**PUBLIC / DEMO — not a real claim. No homeowner, no house, no money. $0.**

This is a fresh case, picked today, Wed Nov 11, 2026 — not Adair, not
Norman, not the South Texas flood. It is the first scenario where the
Weather Service's own warning text says, in plain words, to expect
**wind damage to roofs**.

## The event

A cold front crossed the Ohio Valley on **Wednesday Nov 11, 2026**:

- **Morning — low end.** The Storm Prediction Center's 6:55 AM outlook
  put the area at **Marginal Risk** (level 1 of 5). NWS Wilmington's
  overnight outlook said the chance of widespread bad weather was low
  and spotters probably wouldn't be needed.
- **Afternoon — it climbed.** By 2:00 PM, SPC upgraded part of the
  area to **Slight Risk** (level 2 of 5) over central and southern
  Indiana, western Kentucky and far southeastern Illinois, because of
  15% hail and wind odds. SPC expected scattered severe hail around
  **1 to 1.5 inches**. A mesoscale discussion said storms would form in
  eastern Illinois and spread east through the evening, with hail
  peaking around **1.00 to 1.75 inches**, and gave a 40% chance a
  watch would be issued. NWS Louisville added the same Slight risk,
  saying winds up to 60 mph and near quarter-size hail were the main
  impacts — and marked its confidence **Low**.
- **Evening — the watch and the warnings.** Severe Thunderstorm
  **Watch 704** ran until 11 PM Eastern, over more than 3.8 million
  people, with a couple of tornadoes possible, hail up to ping-pong
  size, and gusts up to 70 mph in the watch graphic.
- **What actually got reported:** trained spotters reported
  **1.0-inch hail near Maceo, Kentucky** (eastern Daviess County) at
  6:23 PM Central. The warning for that storm said 60 mph gusts and
  quarter-size hail, and its impact line read, in full: *"Hail damage
  to vehicles is expected. Expect wind damage to roofs, siding, and
  trees."* Around Indianapolis, radar showed half-inch hail and winds
  over 50 mph in Hendricks County, and a line from Greensburg to
  Bedford with winds over 40 mph. In southeastern Indiana, radar near
  Oldenburg showed half-inch hail and 45 mph gusts at 8:02 PM.
- **It wound down county by county.** Watch 704 was cancelled piece by
  piece from 7:31 PM on, including Indianapolis (Marion County). NWS
  Wilmington cancelled its severe warning for Dearborn, Franklin and
  Ripley counties at 8:36 PM because the storm had weakened below
  severe limits.

## The two hard problems this case caught (and held open)

1. **The hail numbers don't line up.** Forecasts said hail could peak
   at 1.00–1.75 inches, around 1–1.5 inches, or up to ping-pong size
   (1.5 inches), depending on which product you read. What was
   actually reported or radar-indicated was smaller: 1.0 inch by
   spotters near Maceo, half an inch near Oldenburg and around
   Indianapolis. And at build time, the Storm Prediction Center's
   filtered report list for that evening hadn't been published yet.
   The package does **not** pick a winner — it records the gap as an
   open, material conflict. A real claim would need the property-level
   evidence, not the forecast peak.
2. **A tornado warning is not a tornado.** A storm cell in western
   Indiana triggered a tornado warning, then weakened by about
   5:45 PM. Every SPC product for the day listed only a low tornado
   chance, and no tornado report showed up in any product checked.
   This package records a **warning**, not a tornado, and it must
   never be used as tornado-damage evidence.

There is also an honest time line: the risk went **up** during the
day — Marginal in the morning, Slight in the afternoon, watch and
warnings in the evening. That is the weather changing its mind as the
front moved, not a contradiction. The package keeps each product with
its own clock so the globe view shows the same thing.
